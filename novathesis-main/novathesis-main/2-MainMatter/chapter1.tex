\section{Context and Motivation}
\label{sec:intro_context}

As telecommunication networks grow in scale and heterogeneity, routing, quality of
service and resource management become increasingly difficult to handle with
hand-crafted rules. Traditional routing protocols such as OSPF and BGP rely on static,
rule-based decisions that adapt poorly to variable traffic, heterogeneous
Quality-of-Service (QoS) requirements and competing optimisation constraints. Deep
Reinforcement Learning (DRL) has emerged as an alternative, allowing routing agents to
learn adaptive policies directly from observations of the network rather than from
explicitly programmed rules~\cite{Tian2023maddpgslice}.

Multi-agent formulations extend this to distributed control, in which every forwarding
node is an agent that decides locally while the paths chosen collectively determine
end-to-end delivery. Multi-Agent Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (MADDPG) is a
natural fit for this setting: under centralised training with decentralised execution,
each agent learns from a critic that may observe the whole system during training, yet
acts on its own local observation once deployed~\cite{lowe2017multi}. Combined with
Graph Neural Networks, which exploit the graph structure of the topology, such
controllers have been reported to improve scalability, topology awareness and
generalisation across network
configurations~\cite{Almasan_2022,wang2024multi,huang2025era,9974607,zhang2025dramadynamicpacketrouting,11134361}.

The same property that makes these controllers adaptive also exposes them. A learned
routing policy decides from the network state it perceives, and neural policies are
known to be sensitive to small, deliberately chosen perturbations of their
inputs~\cite{szegedy2014intriguingpropertiesneuralnetworks,goodfellow2015explainingharnessingadversarialexamples}.
Attacks such as the Fast Gradient Sign Method
(FGSM)~\cite{goodfellow2015explainingharnessingadversarialexamples}, Projected Gradient
Descent (PGD)~\cite{madry2019deeplearningmodelsresistant} and the Carlini and Wagner
attack~\cite{carlini2017evaluatingrobustnessneuralnetworks} were developed largely for
image classification, where one perturbation targets one prediction. In reinforcement
learning the stakes differ: a perturbed observation changes an action, the action
changes the states that follow, and the damage accumulates over a
trajectory~\cite{zhang2020robust}. For a routing controller, an adversary who can
corrupt the telemetry an agent receives, without ever touching the network itself, can
redirect traffic. How vulnerable learned routing controllers are to such an adversary
remains largely unexplored.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:intro_problem}

This thesis continues a line of research on a MADDPG routing controller developed in
earlier work~\cite{amaral2021maddpg-routing}. A preceding dissertation in the same line
attacked that controller with FGSM, perturbing each agent's observation in a single
gradient step at every time step~\cite{TODO-colleague-fgsm}. The attack altered a
substantial fraction of the routing decisions taken by the victim, yet recovered only
a small part of the degradation achievable in principle, because the redundancy of the
topology absorbed the redirected traffic.

That result admits two readings. Either the controller is genuinely robust, or a
single-step, per-agent adversary is too weak for its failure to count as evidence of
robustness. The two cannot be separated without an attacker that is free of those two
limitations and theoretically stronger. This thesis builds one and compares it with
FGSM on identical episodes and within the same admissible perturbation set. The central
research question is therefore:

\begin{quote}
\emph{Does the apparent robustness of MADDPG routing controllers to FGSM survive a
theoretically stronger observation-space attack, one that coordinates its perturbation
across agents and divides it across time?}
\end{quote}

\noindent It is broken down into four questions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[\textbf{RQ1}] Does dividing the attack across time, spending a limited budget
        on the network's most critical states, buy more damage per attacked step than
        perturbing every step?
  \item[\textbf{RQ2}] Does coordinating the perturbation across compromised agents,
        rather than attacking each agent in isolation, increase the damage?
  \item[\textbf{RQ3}] Which architectural choices of the victim, namely its critic
        domain, its value head and the use of GNN encoding, determine how vulnerable
        it is?
  \item[\textbf{RQ4}] Is the fraction of routing decisions an attack changes a
        meaningful measure of the damage it does?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Approach}
\label{sec:intro_approach}

The attack addresses the two weaknesses of the baseline in turn, drawing on a different
source of inspiration for each. Where FGSM spends its whole perturbation budget in a
single step, PGD divides it into a sequence of smaller projected
steps~\cite{madry2019deeplearningmodelsresistant}. The attack carries this principle
from the input into time: instead of applying a full-strength perturbation at every
step, it acts on only a fraction of the steps, chosen by a critical-state score that
identifies the moments at which the network's redundancy is thinnest. Where the
baseline perturbs each agent in isolation, SAJA~\cite{guo2025sajastateactionjointattack}
judges the perturbations of several victim agents together, by the centralised critic's
value of the joint action they induce. Inspired by it, the attack coordinates its
perturbation jointly across the compromised agents, so that several of them can be
steered towards the same shared link.

The attack is realised as the learned observation adversary of the State-Adversarial
MDP~\cite{zhang2020robust}. Because the victim is frozen, its policies are fixed, and
against a fixed policy the problem of finding the optimal observation adversary is
itself an ordinary Markov decision process. The adversary is therefore trained by
reinforcement learning to act in this coordinated and strategically timed way, learning
which perturbation to apply from the damage its perturbations cause. In principle this
makes it stronger than FGSM, since within the same admissible set the optimal such
adversary is at least as damaging, by its own measure of damage, as any deterministic
per-step perturbation rule, FGSM included. It also knows less about the victim: it
never needs the victim's gradients, only its actions during training. It does, however,
need a faithful simulator of the network to train against and, when its attack is timed,
the utilisation of every link while it attacks.

The attack is evaluated against seven victim variants that differ in their critic
domain, their value head and the use of GNN encoding. To keep the comparison with the
FGSM study meaningful, the attack operates on the same frozen agents and within the
same $\ell_\infty$ perturbation budget, and is scored under the same evaluation
protocol. It is evaluated under the FGSM study's domain constraint and, as a second
condition, under a stricter constraint that keeps every observation feature within
$[0,1]$, with a separately trained adversary for each. Every configuration is run as
three paired arms on identical traffic: a clean arm, a random control that draws a
random perturbation from the same admissible set, and the attack. The damage attributed
to the attack is its margin over the random control, since any perturbation within
that set disturbs the controller whether or not its direction was chosen
adversarially.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:intro_contributions}

The main contributions of this thesis are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item An observation-space attack on MADDPG routing controllers that extends the
        learned adversary of the State-Adversarial MDP~\cite{zhang2020robust} with
        coordination of the perturbation across agents, inspired by SAJA, and division
        of the attack across time, inspired by PGD, together with an explicit threat
        model of what the attacker must know and control.
  \item A critical-state timing gate that spends a limited attack budget on
        high-leverage moments. It includes a tie-rationing rule that makes the
        realised attack rate match the requested budget, which a plain threshold
        cannot do because the critical-state score concentrates on a few repeated
        values.
  \item An evaluation of the learned adversary against a paired random control that
        shares its admissible set and timing gate, which separates the damage
        attributable to the attacker's choice of perturbation from that of a random
        perturbation within the same set, and which reports decisions changed
        separately from delivery lost.
  \item An empirical study across seven victim architectures, both attack scopes and
        two domain constraints, which finds that the architectural choice that appears
        vulnerable depends on the constraint and the scope.
  \item A paired comparison with the FGSM baseline on identical episodes and within
        the FGSM study's admissible set, measured against its own random control.
\end{itemize}

\section{Document Structure}
\label{sec:intro_structure}

The remainder of this document is organised as follows.
Chapter~\ref{cha:literature_review} reviews the relevant literature: deep reinforcement
learning and its application to routing, MADDPG, graph neural networks and their use in
routing, and adversarial attacks on reinforcement learning agents, from FGSM and PGD to
learned State-Adversarial MDP adversaries, strategically-timed attacks and SAJA, and it
closes by identifying the gap this thesis addresses.
Chapter~\ref{cha:methodology} sets out the research approach and experimental design,
the routing system and FGSM baseline inherited from earlier work, the victim
architectures under test, the attack with its SAJA-inspired coordination and
PGD-inspired timing mechanisms and the learned adversary that realises it, and then how
the data were collected and analysed, how the validity of the results was protected,
and the delimitations of the study. Chapter~\ref{chap:results} presents the results under the FGSM study's domain
constraint and under the stricter one, reporting the independent and coordinated
scopes, the comparison with the FGSM baseline, the effect of coordination and the effect
of the constraint itself.
Chapter~\ref{cha:conclusions} concludes, discusses the limitations of the results and
outlines directions for future work. Appendix~\ref{app:triggers} gives the heatmaps of
why the timing gate fired.
