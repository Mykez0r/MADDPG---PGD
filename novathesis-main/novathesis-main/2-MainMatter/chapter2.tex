\chapter{Literature Review}
\label{cha:literature_review}

This chapter reviews the literature on which this thesis builds, organised around its
two subjects: learning-based routing controllers, which are the victims, and adversarial
attacks on reinforcement learning agents, which are the tools used against them. Works
were selected for their relevance to the system under test, a multi-agent routing
controller trained with MADDPG and optionally encoded with a graph neural network, and to
the attack developed here, a learned observation-space adversary with a coordination
mechanism and a timing mechanism. Section~\ref{sec:lit_drl} introduces deep
reinforcement learning and its value-based methods, Section~\ref{sec:lit_maddpg} the
multi-agent algorithm used by the victim and its applications to routing, and
Section~\ref{sec:lit_gnn} graph neural networks and their use in routing.
Section~\ref{sec:lit_attacks} then reviews attacks on reinforcement learning agents,
from single-step gradient attacks to learned, timed and multi-agent attacks, and
Section~\ref{sec:gnn_attacks} briefly situates attacks on graph neural networks.
Section~\ref{sec:lit_summary} draws these strands together and identifies the gap this
thesis addresses.

\section{Deep Reinforcement Learning}
\label{sec:lit_drl}

\textbf{Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL)} combines reinforcement learning with deep
neural networks. An agent interacts with an environment, choosing actions from its
observations and receiving rewards, and learns a policy that maximises its expected
cumulative reward. Because the policy is learned from observations rather than
programmed, DRL suits network optimisation problems whose dynamics are too complex for
hand-crafted rules~\cite{theodoropoulos2023multi,10535707,lozanocuadra2024opensourcemultiagentdeep}.
In routing, DRL agents learn forwarding policies that adapt to congestion, link failures
and changing demand without an explicit model of the
network~\cite{11227800,10730787,11122503}. The problem is usually modelled as a Markov
decision process, or a partially observable one, in which states describe link loads
and topology, actions select next hops or paths, and rewards reflect delay, throughput or
a composite quality-of-service measure~\cite{10684240}.

\subsection{Deep Q-Networks}
\label{sec:dqn}

\textbf{Deep Q-Networks (DQN)} combine Q-learning with a deep neural network that
approximates the action-value function $Q(s,a;\theta)$, taking an observation as input
and producing an estimate of the expected discounted return of every discrete action
as output~\cite{mnih2013playingatarideepreinforcement,mnih2015human}. The agent acts
greedily with respect to these estimates, with occasional random actions for
exploration, and the network is trained to minimise the temporal-difference error
\begin{equation}
L(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(s,a,r,s')}\Big[ \big( r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s', a'; \theta^{-}) - Q(s, a; \theta) \big)^2 \Big],
\end{equation}
where $\gamma$ is the discount factor and $\theta^{-}$ are the parameters of a target
network that is updated only periodically. Two mechanisms make this training stable:
experience replay, which draws the transitions $(s,a,r,s')$ at random from a memory
buffer to break the correlation between consecutive samples, and the slowly updated
target network, which keeps the regression target from shifting at every
update~\cite{mnih2015human}.

Its main advantage is that it learns control policies directly from high-dimensional
observations, without hand-crafted features, and reuses past experience through replay;
this has made it a common basis for learned routing, for example in underwater sensor
networks~\cite{10012647} and in software-defined data centres~\cite{9105030}. Its main
limitations follow from the same design. Because the network outputs one value per
action and the target maximises over them, DQN applies only to discrete action spaces
of moderate size. Training still needs a large amount of interaction with the
environment, and the replay buffer and target network are needed precisely because
combining neural networks with temporal-difference learning is otherwise unstable.

\subsection{Duelling Q-networks}
\label{subsec:duelling_dqn}

The duelling architecture changes only the network of a DQN, not its training
algorithm~\cite{wang2016duelingnetworkarchitecturesdeep}. After a shared set of feature
layers, the network splits into two streams: a value stream $V(s)$ that estimates how
good the state is, and an advantage stream $A(s,a)$ that estimates how much better each
action is than the others in that state. The two are recombined into action values as
\begin{equation}
Q(s,a) = V(s) + \Big( A(s,a) - \frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|} \sum_{a'} A(s,a') \Big),
\end{equation}
where subtracting the mean advantage makes the split between $V$ and $A$ identifiable.

The advantage of this separation is that the value of a state can be learned from every
transition, without having to learn the effect of each action separately. Learning is
therefore more efficient in states where the choice of action matters little, and the
benefit grows with the number of actions that have similar
values~\cite{wang2016duelingnetworkarchitecturesdeep,zhao2025breakingcoldstartbarrierreinforcement}.
This property has been used in routing, for instance to allocate routes and spectrum in
elastic optical networks, where many routing actions are of similar
merit~\cite{ZHANG2022108663}. Its limitations are those of DQN, since it remains a
value-based method for discrete actions, together with a larger network and a gain that
is small when the actions' values differ sharply. The duelling idea also appears in the
victims of this thesis, whose critics use a value and an advantage stream
(Section~\ref{subsec:axis_value_head}), although in a form that differs from the
original.

\section{Multi-Agent Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient}
\label{sec:lit_maddpg}

\textbf{Multi-Agent Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (MADDPG)} extends actor-critic
learning to settings with several agents through \emph{centralised training with
decentralised execution} (CTDE)~\cite{lowe2017multi}. Each agent has an actor that acts
on its own local observation and a critic that, during training only, conditions on the
actions of all agents. Conditioning on the joint action removes the non-stationarity
that each agent would otherwise perceive as its peers' policies change, which is the
main obstacle to training independent learners. Lowe et al.\ also propose two optional
extensions: approximating the other agents' policies from their observed actions, for
when those policies are not known, and training an ensemble of sub-policies per agent to
make policies robust to changing opponents in competitive
settings~\cite{lowe2017multi}.

The same execution model defines the attack surface studied in this thesis. After
training the critics are no longer used, and each actor maps its own observation to an
action with no exchange of information with its peers. An adversary who corrupts the
observations therefore acts on the policies directly, and a corrupted observation at one
agent is not corrected by the others~\cite{10684240,pham2022c,li2025attacking}.

\subsection{MADDPG in network routing}
\label{subsec:lit_maddpg_routing}

MADDPG has been applied to several network control problems. Tian et
al.~\cite{Tian2023maddpgslice} treat each virtual network function of a 5G network slice
as an agent that allocates resources and migrates as demand changes, and report better
performance than approaches that do not use MADDPG. Huang et al.~\cite{huang2025era}
cast routing in software-defined networks as a cooperative multi-agent problem in their
ERA-MADDPG scheme: agents select paths from observed traffic and topology, the
centralised critic uses the global state during training, and the decentralised actors
adapt routes to changing traffic, which the authors find more efficient to train and
faster to recover under topology changes than other DRL methods. RS-MADDPG~\cite{kuang2025rs}
maps each node's local observation to continuous routing decisions, such as splitting
ratios or next hops, and converges about 30 training cycles earlier than its baselines.
DRAMA~\cite{zhang2025dramadynamicpacketrouting} keeps MADDPG's CTDE training but
replaces the critic's fixed joint observation-action input with a Q-network that scores
actions from topology-aware latent features and a graph-based emergent communication
layer, so that the trained network can score newly appearing neighbours and cope with
link failures without retraining; it reports higher delivery rates and lower latency
than standard MADDPG across traffic loads and dynamic topologies.

Taken together, these works agree that CTDE suits routing: a centralised critic
stabilises training, while decentralised actors make fast local decisions that scale
with the network. They differ in the problem addressed, from slice resource allocation to
packet routing, in the action representation, discrete or continuous, and in the
baselines and metrics against which they report gains, so their improvements are not
directly comparable. They also share an assumption: every one of them evaluates its
controller on the observations the network actually produces. None examines how the
learned policies behave when those observations are corrupted, so the robustness of
MADDPG routing controllers to adversarial inputs remains largely unexplored.

\section{Graph Neural Networks}
\label{sec:lit_gnn}

\textbf{Graph Neural Networks (GNNs)} are deep learning models that operate directly on
graph-structured data. Each layer updates a node's representation by aggregating, or
passing messages from, the representations of its neighbours, so that after several
layers every node's representation encodes both its own attributes and the structure of
its surrounding graph, with the same parameters shared across all
nodes~\cite{zhou2021graphneuralnetworksreview,zhang2022gnnreview,wu2021gnnreview,chami2022gnnreview}.
Surveys of the field distinguish several families, including recurrent GNNs, spectral
and spatial convolutional GNNs, graph autoencoders and spatio-temporal GNNs, which trade
off expressivity, scalability and ease of
training~\cite{zhou2021graphneuralnetworksreview,wu2021gnnreview}. This thesis relies on
none of the more specialised variants: the GNN-encoded victims of
Section~\ref{subsec:axis_gnn} use a standard spatial message-passing encoder of two graph
convolution layers with a residual connection.

\subsection{GNN applications in network routing}
\label{subsec:lit_gnn_routing}

GNNs have been applied to routing and traffic engineering along three main
lines~\cite{jianggraph}. The first uses GNNs as fast surrogate models of the network:
Rusek et al.~\cite{rusek2019unveiling} show that message-passing GNNs can estimate
per-link delay and loss accurately enough to evaluate many candidate routing schemes
without full simulation, and later work feeds such predictions into external
optimisers~\cite{jianggraph}. The second couples a GNN encoder with DRL to learn routing
policies directly. G-Routing encodes the topology, traffic matrix and routing state and
passes predicted path-level metrics to a DRL agent, converging faster and behaving more
stably than shortest-path and traditional baselines under changing traffic and
failures~\cite{wei2023grouting}; GROM learns traffic-splitting policies with a GNN and
DRL, improving training stability and generalisation to unseen
topologies~\cite{Ding2024grom}. The third designs GNN architectures around the structure
of the traffic-engineering problem itself, for example by representing paths, demands,
links and the global objective as nodes of one heterogeneous graph, which lets the model
generalise to unseen topologies while approximately respecting
constraints~\cite{zhou2025trafficengineeringlargescalenetworks}. A broader
survey~\cite{jiang2024graph} places these models within GNN-based control and
optimisation, where routing and traffic engineering are among the most studied
applications.

The recurring argument across these works is generalisation: because a GNN's
parameters are shared across nodes, a model trained on one topology can be applied to
another. The same property has a consequence for robustness that is not the focus of
these works. Since each node's representation is computed from its neighbours', a
perturbation of one node's input propagates to the representations of the nodes around
it, so a GNN encoder changes how an attack on one agent's observations reaches the
others.

\section{Adversarial Attacks on Deep Reinforcement Learning}
\label{sec:lit_attacks}

Neural networks are vulnerable to adversarial examples, inputs altered by small,
deliberately chosen perturbations that change the model's
output~\cite{szegedy2014intriguingpropertiesneuralnetworks}. DRL policies inherit this
vulnerability, and attacks against them lead to poor decisions across many application
areas~\cite{alzubaidi2025adversarial,zhou2024adversarial,finlayson2019adversarialexample}.
Attacks introduced at test time are called evasion attacks, and those introduced during
training poisoning attacks. Attacks on multi-agent systems such as MADDPG can further be
classified by the part of the system they perturb (observations, actions, inter-agent
communication, or the training process), by when they act, and by what the attacker
knows about the victim, from full white-box access to its parameters to black-box query
access only. Figure~\ref{fig:maddpg_attack_taxonomy} organises the attacks reviewed below
by the part of the system they perturb.

\subsection{Gradient-based observation attacks: FGSM and PGD}
\label{subsec:observation_attacks}

The \textbf{Fast Gradient Sign Method (FGSM)} perturbs an input $x$ by a single step of
size $\varepsilon$ along the sign of the gradient of the model's loss $J$,
\begin{equation}
\tilde{x} = x + \varepsilon \cdot \operatorname{sign}\big(\nabla_x J(\theta, x, y)\big),
\end{equation}
where $\theta$ are the model's parameters and $y$ the input's
label~\cite{goodfellow2015explainingharnessingadversarialexamples}. Goodfellow et al.\
attribute its effectiveness to the largely linear behaviour of neural networks in high
dimensions, contradicting the earlier view that adversarial vulnerability stems from
their non-linearity, and FGSM has been reported not only to mislead models but to leave
them confident in their mistakes~\cite{villegas2024evaluating}. Its cost of one gradient
evaluation has made it the standard baseline from which many later attacks
developed~\cite{akhtar2021advances}. Kurakin et al.\ apply it iteratively as the Basic
Iterative Method, taking several smaller steps and clipping the result to the
$\varepsilon$-neighbourhood of the input after each, and add a targeted variant that
moves towards the least likely class~\cite{kurakin2016adversarialexamplesphysicalworld};
other extensions target specific classes through the model's internal
features~\cite{rozsa2016adversarial} or improve transfer to unseen models by randomly
transforming the input at each iteration~\cite{Xie_2019_CVPR}.

\textbf{Projected Gradient Descent (PGD)}, introduced by Madry et
al.~\cite{madry2019deeplearningmodelsresistant}, generalises this into a multi-step
attack. Starting from a random point in the allowed perturbation set $S$ around $x$, it
repeatedly takes a signed gradient step of size $\alpha$ and projects the result back
onto $x + S$,
\begin{equation}
x_{t+1} = \Pi_{x+S}\bigl(x_t + \alpha\,\operatorname{sign}(\nabla_x L(\theta, x_t, y))\bigr).
\end{equation}
Madry et al.\ study mainly the $\ell_\infty$ ball, which bounds the change to each input
feature and under which a single step of size $\varepsilon$ recovers FGSM, with
additional experiments under the $\ell_2$ ball. Across many random restarts PGD finds
local maxima of very similar loss, which leads the authors to regard it as a universal
first-order adversary: robustness to PGD indicates robustness to any attack that uses
only gradient information~\cite{madry2019deeplearningmodelsresistant}.

The comparison between the two methods matters for how an attack's failure should be
read. Kurakin et al.\ found that models adversarially trained on single-step examples
can perform better on those examples than on clean inputs, a ``label leaking'' effect
they attribute to the examples being built from the true
label~\cite{kurakin2017adversarialmachinelearningscale,akhtar2021advances}. Madry et al.\
observe that networks trained against FGSM overfit to the narrow set of examples it
produces and show no robustness against PGD, and conclude that robustness must be
judged against a strong multi-step attacker rather than a single-step
one~\cite{madry2019deeplearningmodelsresistant}. A single-step attack that fails
therefore says little about the model's robustness, an argument
Section~\ref{subsec:conclusions_fgsm} returns to once a multi-step attack has been tried
on the victims of this thesis. PGD is one of the two inspirations
for the timing mechanism of this thesis (Section~\ref{subsec:timed_attack}), the other
being the strategically-timed attack of
Lin et al.~\cite{lin2019tacticsadversarialattackdeep} reviewed in
Section~\ref{subsec:lit_timed_attacks}: where FGSM spends its budget in one step and PGD
divides it into several, the attack divides its effort across time, while every attacked
step remains bounded by the full $\varepsilon$.

Both methods were, however, designed for classifiers, where one perturbation targets one
prediction, and they carry three limitations into reinforcement learning. They are
white-box, requiring the gradients of the victim. They choose each perturbation greedily
at a single time step, although a perturbed action changes the states that follow. And
applied to multi-agent learners they perturb each agent separately. Against MADDPG, Guo
et al.\ report that FGSM is of limited effectiveness, because gradients derived from
imperfectly trained critics give unreliable
guidance~\cite{guo2025sajastateactionjointattack,Waghela_2024}. The works reviewed next
each address one of these limitations.

\subsection{Learned adversaries and the State-Adversarial MDP}
\label{subsec:sa_mdp}

In reinforcement learning, a perturbation that alters one action also alters the
trajectory that follows, so its cost is measured in lost cumulative return rather than
in a single incorrect output. Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2020robust} formalise this setting
as the \textbf{State-Adversarial Markov Decision Process (SA-MDP)}. It is defined by the
tuple $(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, B, R, p, \gamma)$, where $\mathcal{S}$, $\mathcal{A}$,
$R$, $p$ and $\gamma$ are the state space, action space, reward function, transition
probability and discount factor of an ordinary MDP, and $B(s) \subseteq \mathcal{S}$ is
the set of perturbations the adversary may apply at state $s$, commonly an
$\ell_p$-norm ball. The adversary is a deterministic, stationary and Markovian function
$\nu: \mathcal{S} \to \mathcal{S}$ with $\nu(s) \in B(s)$. The agent selects its action
from the perturbed observation, $a \sim \pi(\cdot \mid \nu(s))$, but the environment
evolves from the true state $s$, so that
\begin{equation}
\tilde{V}_{\pi \circ \nu}(s) = \sum_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \pi\bigl(a \mid \nu(s)\bigr)
\sum_{s' \in \mathcal{S}} p(s' \mid s, a)
\Bigl[ R(s, a, s') + \gamma \, \tilde{V}_{\pi \circ \nu}(s') \Bigr],
\label{eq:samdp_value}
\end{equation}
and the adversary corrupts only what the agent perceives, never the dynamics or the
reward it receives~\cite{zhang2020robust}.

The central result for attack design is that, for a \emph{fixed} policy $\pi$, finding
the optimal adversary is itself a standard reinforcement learning problem. Zhang et al.\
show (Lemma~1) that there exists an ordinary MDP whose actions are the perturbed
observations presented to the agent and whose reward is the negative of the agent's
reward, and that the optimal policy of this MDP is the optimal adversary $\nu^*$. The
optimal adversary can therefore be learned by an ordinary reinforcement learning
algorithm that treats the victim as part of its environment, without differentiating
the victim, and because it maximises an accumulated reward it takes into account how
each perturbation shapes the states that follow. The framework also explains why
robustness is hard to obtain: under the optimal adversary an optimal Markovian policy
need not exist (Theorem~4), and the loss of value is bounded by how far the policy's
action distribution can be moved within the perturbation set (Theorem~5),
\begin{equation}
\max_{s \in \mathcal{S}} \bigl\{ V_\pi(s) - \tilde{V}_{\pi \circ \nu^*}(s) \bigr\}
\;\le\; \alpha \max_{s \in \mathcal{S}} \, \max_{\hat{s} \in B(s)}
D_{TV}\bigl(\pi(\cdot \mid s), \, \pi(\cdot \mid \hat{s})\bigr),
\label{eq:samdp_bound}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha$ is a state-independent constant and $D_{TV}$ the total variation
distance. This bound motivates state-adversarial regularisers, instantiated as SA-PPO,
SA-DDPG and SA-DQN, of which SA-DDPG is the closest to the MADDPG family. The same work
proposes two attacks that do not need the victim's critic: the Maximal Action Difference
(MAD) attack, which maximises the divergence between the policy's action distributions
on clean and perturbed states, and the Robust Sarsa attack, which first learns an
estimate of the victim's value function and then uses it to guide a gradient
perturbation~\cite{zhang2020robust}.

Zhang et al.\ build on Lemma~1 in \textbf{Alternating Training with Learned
Adversaries (ATLA)}~\cite{zhang2021robust}, which trains an adversary against a fixed
agent by policy gradient, then trains the agent against that adversary, and alternates.
In their experiments the learned adversary is considerably stronger than the per-step
attacks before it, lowering the victim's reward well below critic-based, MAD, Robust
Sarsa and snooping attacks, and it can drive an agent in the opposite direction of its goal rather than
merely halting it. Its weakness is cost: the adversary's action space is the full
observation space, which becomes hard to learn as the observation grows.
\textbf{PA-AD}~\cite{sun2021strongest} addresses this by splitting the adversary into a
director, which learns in which direction to push the policy, and an actor, which crafts
the observation perturbation that realises it; it achieves strong attacks on Atari and
MuJoCo tasks and, used in adversarial training, yields policies more robust than earlier
defences.

These works establish that a learned adversary can be stronger than a gradient attack
while knowing less about the victim, needing only to query its actions and to interact
with the environment. They were, however, developed and evaluated for a single agent,
and do not address settings in which several agents are perturbed at once. The attack of
this thesis takes the learned adversary of the SA-MDP as its basis, applied to a frozen
victim whose policies are fixed, which is exactly the condition of Lemma~1.

\subsection{Strategically-timed attacks}
\label{subsec:lit_timed_attacks}

Gradient attacks on reinforcement learning agents are usually applied at every time
step. Lin et al.~\cite{lin2019tacticsadversarialattackdeep} argue that this ignores the
sequential nature of the problem, since perturbations at different moments are not
equally effective and an attack at every step is also more likely to be detected. Their
\textbf{strategically-timed attack} minimises the agent's expected return subject to a
limit on the number of time steps at which a perturbation is applied. Because solving
this constrained problem exactly is intractable, they decide when to attack with a
heuristic, the gap between the probabilities the policy assigns to its most and least
preferred actions (from a softmax of the Q-values for value-based agents), and attack
only when this gap exceeds a threshold. At the chosen steps, a Carlini and Wagner
attack~\cite{carlini2017evaluatingrobustnessneuralnetworks} drives the agent to its
originally least preferred action. On agents trained with DQN and A3C in five Atari
games, the attack reduced the reward about as much as attacking at every step while
acting on only about a quarter of the steps~\cite{lin2019tacticsadversarialattackdeep}.
The same work introduces the enchanting attack, which plans a sequence of perturbations
with a learned video prediction model to lure the agent to a chosen target state.

The result shows that where an attack is spent can matter as much as how often it is
applied. The method has three limitations that bear on this thesis. Its threshold sets
the attack rate only indirectly, so a requested budget is not guaranteed. Its timing
signal is read from the victim's own policy, which requires access to the policy's
outputs. And it was studied for single agents in games. The timing mechanism of this
thesis addresses the first two by calibrating its threshold online and reading its
signal from the network rather than from the victim (Section~\ref{subsec:timed_attack}).

\subsection{Multi-agent and joint attacks}
\label{subsec:joint_attacks}

\textbf{State-Action Joint Attacks (SAJA)}~\cite{guo2025sajastateactionjointattack} start
from the observation that earlier work attacks either the states or the actions of a
multi-agent system in isolation. SAJA is a white-box attack on multi-agent actor-critic
algorithms with a centralised critic, and it operates in two phases. In the first, the
observations of the victim agents are perturbed by iterated sign-gradient ascent within
an $\ell_\infty$ ball, using both the actor and the critic: the actions $a'$ that the
policies produce from the perturbed observations are scored by the centralised critic,
and the loss combines the negative critic value $-Q(s^{\text{adv}}, a')$ with an
action-difference term $\lVert a' - a^{(0)} \rVert_2$. In the second phase the critic is
used again to perturb the actions themselves. The action-difference term is justified by
a bound showing that the loss of value grows with the multi-agent action difference
(Theorem~2). Evaluated on MADDPG and FACMAC in the Multi-Agent Particle Environment with
one to three victim agents, SAJA reduces the reward by up to 9.90\%, bypasses the
M3DDPG, PA-AD and ATLA defences, and outperforms the corresponding state-only and
action-only attacks~\cite{guo2025sajastateactionjointattack}.

Other multi-agent attacks change the threat model rather than the objective. Attacks on
the action space perturb or replace what agents do: SAJA's action phase applies gradient
ascent on the critic to the actions directly~\cite{guo2025sajastateactionjointattack}.
The Wolfpack attack controls adversarial agents that target one agent and then those
that come to its aid, reducing rewards by up to 51.8\%, severely affecting SMAC
scenarios, and proving far more effective than random or single-agent
attacks~\cite{lee2025wolfpackadversarialattackrobust}.
Adversarial Minority Influence (AMI) lets a minority of adversarial agents sway the
majority towards failure, using a unilateral influence filter and a learned oracle of
harmful target actions; it requires no access to the victims' models and has been
demonstrated on robot swarms, StarCraft II and multi-agent MuJoCo~\cite{li2025attacking}.

These works converge on one point: in multi-agent systems, perturbations chosen for
their combined effect are more damaging than perturbations of individual agents. They
differ in what the attacker must control. SAJA perturbs observations and actions and
needs the victim's centralised critic, while Wolfpack and AMI control entire agents. All
were evaluated in games, particle environments and robotic tasks. The coordination mechanism of
this thesis takes SAJA's central idea, judging the perturbations of several agents by
their joint effect, but keeps to an observation-only threat model and produces the joint
perturbation with a learned adversary rather than with gradients of the victim's critic
(Section~\ref{subsec:coordinated_attack}).

\subsection{Other attack surfaces}
\label{subsec:lit_other_attacks}

Several further attack surfaces lie outside the threat model of this thesis and are
noted only briefly. Systems that communicate explicitly can be attacked through their
messages: an adversarial policy that modifies messages between agents can degrade team
performance several-fold in simulation and by about a factor of two on physical
robots~\cite{wu2023hijacking}, and adaptive attacks on communication persist even when
messages are protected for privacy~\cite{zhao2023dpmac,yuan2023communicationrobustmultiagentlearningadaptable}.
Training-time attacks embed vulnerabilities in the learned policy. Poisoning attackers
can coordinate through MADDPG itself to corrupt a federated model~\cite{shen2021coordinated},
and backdoor attacks implant triggers that make agents take attacker-chosen actions
while behaving normally otherwise, reaching success rates close to 99\% with little loss
of performance on benign
tasks~\cite{dai2025effectivebackdoorattackgraph,guo2025pnact,guo2023policycleanse,chen2022backdoorattacksmultiagentcollaborative}.
Finally, attacks trained on randomised source environments transfer to unseen target
policies, and sometimes outperform white-box attacks on the target, which makes
black-box attacks feasible without knowledge of the victim~\cite{pan2020transferable}.

\section{Adversarial Attacks on Graph Neural Networks}
\label{sec:gnn_attacks}

A separate body of work studies adversarial attacks on GNNs themselves. These attacks
perturb the graph a GNN operates on, by adding, removing or rewiring edges or by
altering node features, in order to mislead node-, link- or graph-level
predictions, either at test time or by poisoning the training
graph~\cite{luo2024survey,wei2020adversarial}. Representative examples are
Nettack~\cite{Nettack}, which perturbs both the structure and the features around a
target node, and Metattack~\cite{metaattackgnn}, which poisons the graph structure
using meta-gradients. In this thesis the GNN serves only as an encoder of the routing
agents' observations, and the attacks perturb those observations rather than the graph
itself, so attacks of this kind are not pursued further.

\section{Summary and Research Gap}
\label{sec:lit_summary}

The literature reviewed in this chapter supports three observations. First,
multi-agent reinforcement learning, and MADDPG in particular, is a well-established
approach to routing, often combined with GNN encoders for their ability to generalise
across topologies, yet these controllers are evaluated only on the observations the
network actually produces. Second, the attack literature agrees that attacks which take
the structure of the problem into account are stronger than single-step attacks applied
at every step: PGD is stronger than FGSM on classifiers, learned SA-MDP adversaries are
stronger than per-step attacks on single agents, strategically-timed attacks match the
damage of every-step attacks at a fraction of the cost, and joint multi-agent
perturbations such as SAJA's outperform perturbations of individual agents. Third, these
stronger attacks were developed and evaluated in games, robotic tasks and particle
environments, and the learned and timed attacks for a single agent, rather than in
networked control systems.

This leaves a gap. No work reviewed here evaluates observation-space attacks against a
multi-agent routing controller, a setting whose path redundancy may absorb redirected
traffic and in which many agents act in parallel. The only such evaluation in this
research line applied FGSM, a single-step, per-agent attack, and found that it did
little damage~\cite{martins2026fgsm}; by the argument of Madry et al., that failure
is weak evidence of robustness. Whether the controller withstands an attacker that is
stronger in principle, a learned SA-MDP adversary that coordinates its perturbation
across agents, as SAJA suggests, and divides its effort across time, as PGD and the
strategically-timed attack of Lin et al.~\cite{lin2019tacticsadversarialattackdeep}
suggest, has not been tested. This thesis addresses that
question, through the research questions set out in Section~\ref{sec:intro_problem}.
