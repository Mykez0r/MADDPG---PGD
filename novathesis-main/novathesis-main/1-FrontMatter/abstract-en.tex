Routing controllers trained with Multi-Agent Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient
(MADDPG), often combined with graph neural networks, decide where to send traffic from
telemetry an attacker may be able to corrupt. This dissertation asks whether the
apparent robustness such a controller showed against the Fast Gradient Sign Method
(FGSM) survives a stronger attacker: the earlier attack perturbed each agent in
isolation and at every time step, so its limited effect on delivery cannot distinguish a
robust controller from an attack too weak to expose it.

The attack built here is the learned observation adversary of the State-Adversarial
Markov Decision Process, extended with two mechanisms: perturbations chosen jointly for
all compromised agents, as SAJA suggests, and divided across time under a budget spent
where the network's redundancy is thinnest, as PGD and strategically-timed attacks
suggest. Twenty-eight adversaries, one per victim variant, attack scope and domain
constraint, were trained against seven frozen MADDPG variants and evaluated on 30 paired
episodes against an unperturbed arm and a random control from the same admissible set,
at four timing budgets and without one, and compared with FGSM on 15 shared episodes.

The learned adversary out-damaged the baseline in one of the 70 comparable
configurations, and coordination and timing each changed which victims were harmed. The
effects are small: the largest reaches under a third of the damage available to an ideal
observation-space attacker, and most are of the same order as the movement a
perturbation of this size produces with no adversarial direction. With one training run
per configuration, the differences between configurations cannot be separated from the
variation between training runs. The evidence therefore supports the weaker claim that
no attack in this family did substantial damage to these controllers, rather than
establishing that they are robust.
